Operators and axes

Combine functions and work along axes

An operator takes one or two values, called operands, and produces a derived function. For example, / turns its operand + into the summing function +/. We’ll use operators to choose axes, combine functions and repeat calculations.

Axes and agreement

mat←3 2⍴⍳6
mat
0 1
2 3
4 5

Agreement is how the axes of two arguments line up. BPL uses leading agreement, matching axis 0 with axis 0, axis 1 with axis 1, and so on. Here the three values on the left line up with the three rows of the matrix. Each value is compared with every item in its row.

0 3 4=mat
$t $f
$f $t
$t $f
mat←[10 20 30⋄40 50 60]
mat
10 20 30
40 50 60

Plus follows the same agreement rule. Add 1 to the first row and 2 to the second.

mat+1 2
11 21 31
42 52 62

To work along chosen axes, use the Axis operator ⍠. Its left operand is a function and its right operand is an axis number or list of axes. +⍠1 applies Plus to the rows, along axis 1. Each row is paired with the whole right-hand vector.

mat+⍠1[1 2 3]
11 22 33
41 52 63

An inverse can work along the same chosen axis. This subtracts the fixed vector from each row, undoing the addition above.

1 2 3+⁻¹⍠1[11 22 33⋄41 52 63]
10 20 30
40 50 60

⍨ Self / Commute

Swapping arguments

Commute swaps a function’s arguments. 3-⍨2 means 2-3.

3-2
1
2-3

¯1

Commute’s inverse undoes the swapped call. Keeping 3 fixed on the left, the inverse finds the 2 that gave the result ¯1.

3-⍨⁻¹¯1

Replicate takes its mask on the left. With Commute, we can write the data first and then calculate its mask. The mask ≠v marks the first occurrence of each value.

v←22 10 22 22 21 10 5 10

With one argument, Self passes that argument to both sides of the function. ×⍨3 means 3×3.

A seed on the right

A seed is a starting value for a reduction. Supply it as the left argument; it goes at the right end of the inserted expression. 10+/1 2 3 is 1+2+3+10.

↕ Windows

Windows makes overlapping groups of consecutive major cells. With a vector, a size of 3 gives a matrix whose rows are the length-3 windows.

3↕1 2 3 4
1 2 3
2 3 4

With no left argument, Windows gives adjacent pairs.

↕1 2 3 4
1 2
2 3
3 4

Reduce each window to get a moving sum.

+/3↕1 2 3 4
6 9

For a moving average, divide those sums by the window size. Commute lets us put the 3 before the expression we divide by it.

3÷⍨+/3↕1 2 3 4
2 3

Windows’ inverse joins overlapping windows back into one array. Their overlapping items must agree. Keep the same size for the dyadic form.

↕⁻¹[1 2⋄2 3⋄3 4]
3↕⁻¹[1 2 3⋄2 3 4]

\ Scan

Running totals

Scan starts with the first item, then combines each result with the next item, working left to right. Plus gives running totals.

+\1 2 3 4 5
1 3 6 10 15
×\1 2 3 4 5
1 2 6 24 120
÷\1 2 3
1 0.5 0.1666666667

The last division above is (1÷2)÷3. Compare that with Reduce’s 1÷(2÷3). A scan can also start from a seed.

10-\1 2 3
9 7 4

A superscript applies to the whole derived function on its left. Scan’s inverse recovers the input from its running results. A seeded scan’s inverse needs the same seed.

10-\⁻¹9 7 4
1 2 3
1 2 3

∘ Atop

Atop applies the function on the right, then applies the function on the left to its result. f∘g y is f(g y); with two arguments, x f∘g y is f(x g y).

Atop’s inverse undoes its two steps in reverse order. Here negating a reciprocal is undone by negating, then taking the reciprocal.

↣ Before and ↢ After

Binding an argument

Binding fixes one of a function’s arguments, leaving the other for a later call. After binds an argument on the right. *↢2 squares its argument.

sqr←*↢2
sqr 3
9

Before binds an argument on the left. 2↣* raises 2 to its argument.

pow_2←2↣*
pow_2 3
8

Inverses apply to bound functions too. 2↣×⁻¹ undoes doubling. A superscript touching a final number would instead apply to that number: +↢3⁻¹ adds a third. Use parentheses, or a space before ⁻¹, to invert the whole bound function.

2↣×⁻¹10
(+↢3)⁻¹10
+↢3 ⁻¹ 10

Combining functions

With functions on both sides, After uses its argument on the left and transforms it on the right. f↢g y means y f(g y). We can scale a list by dividing it by its maximum.

Repeating a function

Power applies a function a given number of times. inc⍣3 start means inc inc inc start. A zero count returns the starting value.

inc⍣3 start

On a function, a superscript digit is a repeat count. inc²5 increments twice, giving 7. On a number, it is an arithmetic power: 5² is 25.

Iterating to a fixed point

A fixed point is a value that a function leaves unchanged. This function adds 1 to the reciprocal of its argument. Repeating it approaches the golden ratio, about 1.618.

f←{1+÷⍵}
f 1
2
f 2
1.5
f 1.5
1.666666667

After 15 steps, the approximation is already close.

f⍣15(1)
1.618034448

The literal ∞ means infinity. As a Power count, it repeats until successive results match. Float comparisons allow a small relative difference, called the tolerance.

f⍣∞(1)
1.618033989

Keeping the intermediate values

A list of counts keeps the result after each count. Counts from 0 include the starting value.

A predicate as the right operand stops Power when it returns true. It sees the new value as ⍺ and the previous value as ⍵.

Linear algebra

. Inner product

Pair, then reduce

The function on the right combines paired items. The function on the left reduces those results. A dot product multiplies each pair and adds the products.

1 2 3+.×4 5 6 is (1×4)+(2×5)+(3×6).

1 2 3+.×4 5 6
32

We can also compare each item with 3, then combine the answers with And.

3∧.=3 3 3 3
$t

For matrices, each row on the left pairs with each column on the right. Their dot products form the matrix product.

mat←[1 2⋄3 4]
mat+.×mat

15 22

⊗ Outer product

Outer product combines every item on the left with every item on the right. Here each left item multiplies the whole right vector, giving one row of a multiplication table.

12 15 18 21

⌹ Matrix inverse / Matrix divide

Inverting a matrix

Exact inputs give rational results for the inverse and linear solve below. Their products have no floating-point roundoff.

mat←[1 2⋄3 4]ₓ
inv←⌹mat
inv
¯2ₓ   1ₓ
3ᵣ2 ¯1ᵣ2

Multiplying the inverse by the original matrix gives the identity matrix. It has 1s on its main diagonal, from top-left to bottom-right, and 0s elsewhere. Multiplying by it leaves a matrix unchanged.

Solving a linear system

A linear system asks which vector x gives a known vector b when multiplied by a known matrix a. Matrix divide b⌹a solves it, finding x such that a+.×x is b. Without an exact solution, it gives a least-squares solution, minimizing the sum of squared differences from b.

solution←[5 6]ₓ⌹mat
solution
¯4ₓ 9ᵣ2
mat+.×solution
[5 6]ₓ

Matrix inverse is its own inverse: ⌹⁻¹ applies ⌹ again.

Custom operators

A derivative measures how quickly a function’s result changes near a point. We can approximate it by dividing the difference between two nearby results by the distance between their arguments. Here d is that distance, called the step size. A smaller step brings the estimate for square at 3 closer to 6.

f←*↢2
d←0.01
x←3
d÷⍨(f x+d)-f x
6.01
d←0.0001
d÷⍨(f x+d)-f x
6.0001

Use ⍶ inside a dfn to make an operator. It names the left operand. This operator takes the point as ⍺ and the step size as ⍵.

grad←{((⍶ ⍺+⍵)-⍶ ⍺)÷⍵}
3 f grad 0.01
6.01

With Commute, we can put the step size first in the division.

grad←{⍵÷⍨(⍶ ⍺+⍵)-⍶ ⍺}
3 f grad 0.01
6.01

A two-operand operator uses ⍹ for its right operand. This one applies its right operand, then its left, like Atop.

compose←{⍶ ⍹ ⍵}
- compose square 3
¯9

Giving a function its inverse

For a dfn, attach an inverse to its name with f⁻¹←g. Here shift adds 3, and we supply the dfn that subtracts 3.

shift←{⍵+3}
shift⁻¹←{⍵-3}
shift⁻¹10
7

∂ Derivative

The built-in Derivative operator differentiates the operations inside its operand. It needs no step size.

f∂3
6

{+/⍵×⍵}∂1 2 3
