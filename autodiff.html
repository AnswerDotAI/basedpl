Automatic differentiation

f∂X gives the derivative of f at X. ∂ works by running f. It differentiates through the primitives that f calls, inside dfns, trains and operators alike. A function needs no special form to be differentiated. ⊼ squares its argument. +/⊼ sums the squares:

⊼∂3                          ⍝ 6
+/⊼ ∂ 1 2 3                 ⍝ 2 4 6

The ∂ page lists what ∂ differentiates and the errors it gives.

What ∂ gives

The Jacobian of f at X holds the derivative of each item of f X with respect to each number in X. f∂X gives it, with the keys, axis names and nesting of the outer product (f X)×⊗X. A function that gives a number has a Jacobian with X’s structure: its gradient. A record argument gives a record gradient, with the record’s keys. cart converts polar coordinates to Cartesian ones. Its Jacobian has a row for each result and a column for each argument:

cart←{[r t]←ω ◇ [r×2○t r×1○t]} ◇ cart∂ 2 0.5   ⍝ [0.8775825619 ¯0.9588510772◇0.4794255386 1.755165124]
p←["w":2 "b":1] ◇ {3×ω.w + ω.b}∂ p               ⍝ ["w":3 "b":1]

W f∂ X differentiates W f X with respect to X. The left argument holds what isn’t differentiated, such as training data:

3 {α×ω×ω}∂ 2                ⍝ 12

A JVP is the derivative of f at x in a direction t. {f x+ω×t}∂0 computes it by differentiating along one number. A vector–Jacobian product multiplies a vector u by the Jacobian. {+/u×fω}∂ x computes it by differentiating a sum. Neither builds the Jacobian. With t and u both 1 0, the JVP gives the Jacobian’s first column. The vector–Jacobian product gives its first row:

f←{[x y]←ω ◇ [x×y x+y]} ◇ x←2 3
f∂ x                        ⍝ [3 2◇1 1]
t←1 0 ◇ {f x+ω×t}∂0         ⍝ 3 1
u←1 0 ◇ {+/u×fω}∂ x         ⍝ 3 2

For a function that works item by item, such as the sine 1○, f∂⁰ gives the derivative at each item. Differentiating along one number gives the same values in one pass:

x←0 0.5 1
1○∂⁰ x                     ⍝ 1 0.8775825619 0.5403023059
{1○x+ω}∂0                   ⍝ 1 0.8775825619 0.5403023059

f∂∂ is ∂ applied to f∂. It gives second and mixed derivatives, such as the matrix of mixed derivatives of ×/, the product of the items. Its derivatives are floats, whatever the kind of X:

{ω×ω×ω}∂∂∂2                  ⍝ 6
×/∂∂ 2 3                     ⍝ [0 1◇1 0]
⊼∂3ₓ                         ⍝ 6

Forward mode

Where X holds one number, ∂ runs f once, in forward mode. Every value that depends on X carries its derivative with respect to X beside it, as a dual number. ∂ starts the pass by making X a dual whose derivative is 1. A call on duals gives a dual. A pervasive function, such as × or *, finds its result’s derivative by the chain rule, from a table of its partial derivatives. α×ω has the partials ω and α. *ω has the partial *ω, its own result. ∂ returns the derivative that the final result carries.

Each ∂ call tags the duals it makes. A call reads only the derivatives with its own tag. Under nested ∂, as in {ω×ω×ω}∂∂∂2, a value is a dual of a dual of a dual. Julia’s ForwardDiff tags its dual numbers in the same way.

Functions that move items, such as reshape, transpose, take and windows, move each item’s derivative with the item. Comparisons, floor, integer ÷, ι, grade and other functions whose results are counts, positions, Booleans or floored integers give results with no derivative.

Reverse mode

Forward mode takes one pass for each number in X. A network with a million parameters would take a million passes. Where X holds more than one number, ∂ instead runs f once with a record in place of each derivative. A record’s items are never computed. Each call on a record adds a linear step to it, built from the same partial derivatives that forward mode uses. The steps are few: scaling by a coefficient, adding, copying from positions, broadcasting, folding by +, and matrix products with a constant. When f returns, the record holds the linearisation of f at X. That’s the linear map from a small change in X to the change it causes in f X.

∂ then reads the Jacobian from the record. A run through the record backwards, through each step’s transpose in reverse order, gives one row of the Jacobian. A run forwards gives one column. ∂ takes the direction that needs fewer runs. A function that gives a number, such as a loss, needs one backward run. Its gradient costs a small multiple of one run of f, however many numbers X holds. Dex and JAX also build reverse mode as the transpose of a linearisation. One set of tangent rules then serves both modes.

Each linear step has one transpose:

Step Transpose
Scaling by a coefficient Scaling by the same coefficient
Adding two values The same gradient to each value
A broadcast A sum over the broadcast axes
A fold by + along an axis A broadcast along that axis
A structural copy or a selection, such as a transpose, reverse, windows or a selection that repeats positions Each item’s gradient, added back where the item was read
A matrix product with a constant A matrix product with the constant’s transpose

The transposes work on whole arrays, as the steps do. The transpose of a copy or a selection adds each gradient into the position that it was read from. It never builds an array of zeros with a one at each position read. In this example, 3↕ gives windows of 3. The first function reads item 0 twice and never reads item 1. In the second, the middle items appear in more of the windows:

0 0 2 {+/⊼ω.[α]}∂ 1 2 3     ⍝ 4 0 6
{+/,⊼3↕ω}∂ 1 2 3 4 5        ⍝ 2 8 18 16 10

Folds and matrix functions have rules of their own. ×/ divides the product by each item to find that item’s derivative, with zeros counted apart. ⌈/ and ⌊/ take the derivative of the item that they pick. ⌹ and the determinant -.× take their derivatives from one factorization of the matrix.

The record keeps the values that its steps need, such as the coefficients of its scalings, until ∂ returns. Its memory grows with the work that f does.

Rules of your own

f∂←g attaches g as the derivative of f. g is a JVP rule: T g X gives the derivative of f at X in the direction T. Forward mode calls g with the tangent as T. Reverse mode records the steps that g takes on T, then transposes them, as JAX transposes a custom_jvp rule. One rule serves both modes. A straight-through estimator, which treats rounding as if its derivative were 1, is such a rule:

r←{ω×ω} ◇ r∂←{α×2×ω}
{1+rω}∂3                    ⍝ 6
ste←{⌊ω} ◇ ste∂←{α}
{+/steω×2}∂ 1.5 2.5         ⍝ 2 2

•detach X gives X with every derivative removed. ∂ then treats it as a constant, as JAX treats the result of stop_gradient. ×↢•detach multiplies its argument by a detached copy of itself. Its derivative at 3 is the copy’s value, 3, where ⊼∂3 gives 6:

×↢•detach ∂3                ⍝ 3

A value that f writes to a name outside itself loses its derivatives when ∂ returns. A function gives the same values with and without ∂.

Writes

∂ differentiates indexed, sliced and masked replacement and additive updates. The replacement can depend on the differentiated input:

{v←ω ◇ v.[0]←0 ◇ +/v}∂1 2 3               ⍝ 0 1 1
{v←ω ◇ v.[0]+←ω.[1] ◇ +/v}∂1 2 3         ⍝ 1 2 1
{v←ω ◇ v.[[0 0]]+←ω.[1] ◇ +/v}∂1 2 3     ⍝ 1 3 1
{v←ω ◇ v.[[0 2]]←+/ω ◇ +/v}∂1 2 3        ⍝ 2 3 2

Positions and masks are fixed choices during differentiation. ∂ does not differentiate which positions were chosen. An additive update counts each addition at a repeated destination, as the third example shows. A broadcast replacement’s gradient sums back to the replacement’s original shape, as the fourth example shows.

These rules also apply to selecting Under.

Programs: trace once, replay

Without a program, ∂ builds its duals or its record afresh on every call, through the interpreter. A training loop calls ∂ hundreds of times, with arguments of the same types and shapes. This loop fits a network with 16 tanh units (7○) to 64 points of sin 3x (1○3×x), in 200 steps of gradient descent on a record of parameters:

x←ι64 ÷ 63 ◇ y←1○3×x
loss←{[x y]←α ◇ [w b v c]←ω ◇ h←7○b(+¹)x×⊗w ◇ +/÷≢ ⊼y-c+h+.×v}
p←["w":1○ι16 "b":16ρ0 "v":0.1×2○ι16 "c":0]
[x y] loss p                ⍝ 0.5232276475
p←{ω - 0.1× [x y]loss∂ω}⍣200p
[x y] loss p                ⍝ 0.07655752909

∂ runs from a program. The first call with given argument types and shapes traces the function. The function runs on traced values, which have a type and a shape but no items. Each call on a traced value works out the type and shape of its result, and adds a step to the program. A map in the function, such as Rank in +/{⊼1○+/w×ω}¹ X, calls its dfn once on all its cells, as it does outside a trace. Each call in the dfn then adds one step for all the cells. The derivative’s records and transposes add their steps to the same program. The program holds the whole call: the function’s value and its derivative together. JAX traces a Python function into a jaxpr in the same way.

Later calls with the same argument types and shapes replay the program on their arguments, without running the function. The replay runs the program’s steps as ordinary calls. Chains of elementwise steps then run as one stage, a block of items at a time, as they do outside a program. A stage folds its last step’s items as it computes them. Its intermediate results exist only one block at a time. Once a stage is large enough, or runs again, BPL compiles it to machine code with Cranelift, if its steps can compile. The browser build has no compiler. It runs every stage on the interpreter’s kernels. •explain shows whether a call built a program or replayed one:

sq←{+/⊼ω} ◇ g←sq∂ 1 2 3
(•explain "sq∂ 4 5 6").programs.event      ⍝ ["replayed"]
(•explain "sq∂ 4 5 6 7").programs.event    ⍝ ["built"]

The second call replays the program that the first call built. Four numbers need a program of their own.

A dfn keeps its programs with its definition. Any other function, such as a train, keeps them by its structure for the rest of the session. The structure leaves out the arrays that the function binds, which become inputs, as a dfn’s free names do. So +/w× replays its program when w holds new values of the same type and shape:

w←1 2 3 ◇ g←+/w× ∂ 4 5 6
w←7 8 9 ◇ (•explain "+/w× ∂ 4 5 6").programs.event   ⍝ ["replayed"]

What stops a trace

A trace stops at anything that a program can’t hold: output or another effect, a random draw, ⍎, a read of a differentiated value, as a guard on one makes, a differentiated value inside a nested array, as enclosing one makes, or a call that programs don’t take yet. ∂ then runs the call through duals and records. ∂ keeps the stop for those argument types and shapes. •explain gives the reason in its stopped rows:

pos←{+/ω > 0 ? +/⊼ω ; 0}
(•explain "pos∂ 1 2 3").stopped.reason    ⍝ ["a read of a traced value"]
(•explain "{+/,ω×⊃(⊂+/ω)×2}∂ 1 2 3").stopped.reason   ⍝ ["a traced value inside a nested array"]

Each replay reads the current value of every name that the function reads from outside its body, and of every array that a function other than a dfn binds. A name or an array read as positions, a count or a guard’s condition is part of the program itself. A replay checks its value. A new value traces the function again.

A program fixes every type before it runs. Under the default dynamic kinds, a result’s type can depend on its values: √ of a negative number gives a complex number. A trace stops at such a call, unless the argument is known to be nonnegative, as ⊼x is. Under static kinds, which •prefs ["kinds":"static"] sets, every type follows from the argument types. The same call then traces. Static kinds are the setting for work where speed matters.

roots←{+/√ω}
(•explain "roots∂ 1 2 3").stopped.reason  ⍝ ["a result whose type depends on values"]

Write a function for ∂ under static kinds, with each branch as Agenda (⍚) or Under at positions (f@[p]) rather than a guard. A guard reads a differentiated value to pick one body for the whole call, which a program can’t hold. ⍚ and @ pick items within whole arrays. Today they also stop a trace where their choice or their values come from differentiated values. ∂ still gives the right result there, more slowly. Tracing them is planned, as “Not built yet” describes.

Index notation

Index notation writes the formula for one element with named indices. BPL runs the formula once, on whole arrays. Indexing and •index describe it. ∂ differentiates index notation as it differentiates any other code. This matrix product, written with indices, has the same gradient as +.×:

xu←[2 3]ρι6 ◇ wu←[3 4]ρι12 ◇ [b o k]←•index 2 4 3
{+/,⊼ω.[b k]+.×⍠k⁝wu.[k o]}∂ xu    ⍝ [324 1108 1892◇1008 3376 5744]
{+/,⊼ω+.×wu}∂ xu                     ⍝ [324 1108 1892◇1008 3376 5744]

In a program, the indices keep their structure:

  • A + fold over named axes of a product, where both operands step along every folded axis, runs as a matrix product +.×. The folds in its gradient run as products where they meet the same condition.
  • A read through positions computed from indices, such as x.[i+1], is a copy through a map. Its transpose reads back through the inverse map.
  • A read through positions from data, such as an embedding lookup E.[ids], is a gather. Its transpose adds into the positions that it read.

Each replay of a dfn that uses •index gives its result’s indices fresh names, as an untraced call does.

BPL’s design studied Dex, whose index sets informed •index. Two points from Dex’s paper hold in BPL’s programs. Only nonlinear steps keep values for the transpose. Indexing is linear and keeps none. The transpose of indexing is an accumulation.

Where the design comes from

JAX, Dex and tinygrad are the role models. Futhark, Dex and Julia set the standard that the design is judged by:

  • A gradient costs a small multiple of one run of the function, whatever the number of variables.
  • The derivative of a whole-array operation is a whole-array operation.
  • One rule for each primitive serves both modes.
  • Nested ∂ never confuses its levels.
  • Code that isn’t differentiated pays nothing for ∂.
System What BPL shares with it
JAX A function traced into a program of primitives, and replayed on later calls. Reverse mode as the transpose of a linearisation. JVP rules that reverse mode transposes, like custom_jvp rules. •detach as stop_gradient. Rank doing the job of vmap.
Dex Linearising, then transposing. Indices that name a formula’s axes. Accumulation as the transpose of indexing.
tinygrad Lazy arrays: a call on a large array gives a pending array, and a read of its items runs the calls it needs, fused. Derivative code that fuses as other code does. Copies, such as a transpose or a slice, whose transposes are copies, not scatters. A program replayed as TinyJit replays its kernels.
Futhark One table of partial derivatives for both modes, which BPL’s table was mapped from. The rules for folds by ×, ⌈ and ⌊. Accumulation as the transpose of a gather.
ForwardDiff Dual numbers with tags, which keep nested derivatives apart.
J D., which gives a Jacobian, and d., which gives the derivative at each item. f∂ and f∂⁰ do these jobs.

BPL also differs from all of them. ∂ strips its tags from values that escape it, where JAX raises an error when a leaked tracer is used. One operator picks the mode, where JAX offers jvp, vjp, jacfwd and jacrev. A Jacobian takes its keys, axis names and records from the rules of outer products. Futhark differentiates its program before running it and recomputes values in reverse mode. BPL differentiates the calls that f makes as it runs. It keeps their linear steps instead of recomputing values.

Not built yet

These are planned:

  • Branches in programs. Agenda (⍚) with a selector computed from differentiated values stops a trace, as a guard on them does. The plan is to trace such a selector as a masked select, as JAX traces select.
  • Programs inside maps. ∂ inside a map, as in {f∂ω}¨x, runs without a program. •explain gives the reason “a call under another level”.
  • Writes. @ and selective assignment with differentiated values stop a trace. ∂ still gives the right result, more slowly.
  • Whole training steps. A program holds one call of ∂. The update, and the loop around it, run outside the program. A tracing point for whole training steps, such as ⍣, would run a step’s forward pass, gradient and update as one program.
  • Deferred Jacobians. f∂ x builds every item of the Jacobian. The plan holds a large Jacobian as its linearisation, and builds its items only when something reads them. J+.×v would then run as a JVP, u+.×J as a vector–Jacobian product, and b⌹J as an iterative solve, as in the Gauss–Newton step x - r⁝x ⌹ r∂x.
  • The GPU. Programs run on the CPU. A later stage adds a WebGPU back end, with arrays kept on the device.

These limits have no plan yet:

  • Reverse mode keeps every value that its transposes need until ∂ returns. A long iteration keeps a record for each step. Checkpointing, which Futhark uses for loops, recomputes values instead of keeping them. BPL has none.
  • Nested ∂, as in f∂∂, runs without a program.
  • ∂ differentiates through each iteration of ⍣∞ and ⇄. Rules from the implicit function theorem would differentiate their answers directly.
  • ∂ gives UNSUPPORTED ERROR for complex numbers.
  • Forward mode carries one direction for each pass.
