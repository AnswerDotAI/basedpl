Composing functions

A tacit function combines other functions without naming its arguments. It often reads as a description of what it computes: +/÷≢ is the sum divided by the count. Where several forms give the same function, write the one that shows its meaning most directly.

The token separator ⁝ keeps names and literals distinct without separating their run.

Trains

A run that ends in a function is a train. A train of two functions is an atop: f⁝g Y is f(g Y). A train of three functions is a fork: f⁝g⁝h Y is (f Y)g(h Y). With a left argument, X(f g)Y is f(X g Y) and X(f g h)Y is (X f Y)g(X h Y). Trains gives the rules for longer trains.

mean←+/÷≢ ⋄ mean 2 4 9       ⍝ 5
1 2 (⊣+⊢) 3 4                ⍝ 4 6

In a train, a subject becomes the left argument of the function after it. 32+1.8× converts Celsius to Fahrenheit by multiplying by 1.8, then adding 32.

fahrenheit←32+1.8× ⋄ fahrenheit 100     ⍝ 212

A space keeps a train apart from its argument. +/÷≢ v applies the train to v. Without the space, the one run +/÷≢v reads as +/÷(≢v):

v←2 4 9 ⋄ +/÷≢ v             ⍝ 5
v←2 4 9 ⋄ +/÷≢v              ⍝ 1ᵣ3

Identity functions

In a train, ⊢ stands for the right argument and ⊣ for the left. A fork with ⊢ reads well where the argument appears once. ⊢÷+/ divides each item by the sum:

⊢÷+/ 1 2 5                 ⍝ 0.125 0.25 0.625
2 (⊢-⊣) 5                    ⍝ 3

Where a formula uses its argument more than once, each ⊢ makes the reader track the argument again. A function that removes the repeats reads better. Newton’s step towards √2 adds half of x to the reciprocal of x. ⊽⁻¹, the inverse of double, halves:

n←⊽⁻¹+÷ ⋄ n 1.5              ⍝ 1.416666667

0.5×⊢+2÷⊢ gives the same results, but its two ⊢s hide the formula. Where no clear tacit form exists, a dfn names the argument, as in {0.5×⍵+2÷⍵}.

Atop and Over

Atop, f∘g, applies g and then f. X f∘g Y is f(X g Y). ⌊∘÷ divides, then rounds down:

7 ⌊∘÷ 2                      ⍝ 3ₓ

Over, f⍥g, applies g to each argument before f. X f⍥g Y is (g X)f(g Y). =⍥≢ tests whether two lists have the same length:

"abc" =⍥≢ "xyz"              ⍝ $t

Before and After

Before, A↣f, binds A as the left argument of f. In a train, a subject binds the same way without the glyph: 2× is 2↣×. After, f↢A, binds A as the right argument. ,↢1 appends 1 and -↢10 subtracts 10:

,↢1⁝2 3                     ⍝ 2 3 1
-↢10⁝3                      ⍝ ¯7

With two function operands, ↣ and ↢ form monadic hooks. f↣g Y is (f Y)g Y and f↢g Y is Y f(g Y). Two-function trains in BPL are atops. (3<)↣# Y is (3<Y)#Y. It keeps the items of Y that are greater than 3:

(3<)↣# 2 7 1 8               ⍝ 7 8

Self and Commute

Self, f⍨Y, is Y f Y. Commute, X f⍨Y, is Y f X:

,⍨"ab"                       ⍝ "abab"
2-⍨10                        ⍝ 8

For a power, a superscript reads more directly than Self: x² rather than ×⍨x.

x←3 ⋄ x²                     ⍝ 9

Inverse

f⁻¹ is the inverse of f, as Superscripts describes. BPL infers the inverses of most compositions. fahrenheit⁻¹ converts back to Celsius:

fahrenheit←32+1.8× ⋄ fahrenheit⁻¹ 212   ⍝ 100

A superscript straight after a number applies to the number. To invert a function that ends in a number, put the function in parentheses:

(+↢3)⁻¹ 10                   ⍝ 7

Without the parentheses, +↢3⁻¹ 10 reads 3⁻¹ as a third. The third strands with 10 into the bound operand. No argument remains. The result is the function +↢[0.3333333333 10].

Under

Under, f@g, applies f to what g selects and writes the result back. (10×)@[1 3] multiplies items 1 and 3 by 10. ⌊@(10×) rounds down to tenths: it scales by 10, rounds down, then scales back.

(10×)@[1 3] 1 2 3 4          ⍝ 1 20 3 40
⌊@(10×)1.25 2.78             ⍝ 1.2 2.7

Separating an operand from its argument

Within a run, an operator’s right operand is the one token after it. A run that ends in a dyadic operator takes the whole next run as its right operand: in ⌊@ 10× x, the operand of @ is 10×. Literals separated by spaces join into one strand before an operator takes its operand, as Syntax describes. ≥⍣3 5 therefore takes 3 5 as its operand and returns the function ≥⍣[3 5].

⁝ (Alt-:) separates tokens without ending the run or continuing a literal strand. Use it between an operand and the derived function’s right argument where they would otherwise merge. It works inside a longer expression too:

≥⍣3⁝5                                       ⍝ 8
m←["r":2 "c":3]⍴⍳6ₓ ⋄ i←"c" ⋄ +/⍠i⁝m         ⍝ ["r":2]⍴[3 12]ₓ
m←["r":2 "c":3]⍴⍳6ₓ ⋄ i←"c" ⋄ 10÷√1++/⍠i⁝m  ⍝ ["r":2]⍴[5 2.773500981]
(2×)⍣3⁝5                                    ⍝ 40
(+/÷≢)⍤1⁝2 3⍴⍳6                              ⍝ 1 4

Add no separator where the tokens already stay distinct. A name never joins a literal strand, as in f⍣n1. A vector argument can touch its operand in brackets, as in ≥⍣2[1 2 3].

⁝ works the same way with a left argument. Parentheses around the derived function also show it between its arguments. Use whichever reads better. Compound operands keep their own parentheses:

w←[1 2⋄3 4⋄5 6] ⋄ 10 20+⍤1⁝w                 ⍝ [11 22⋄13 24⋄15 26]
w←[1 2⋄3 4⋄5 6] ⋄ 10 20(+⍤1)w                ⍝ [11 22⋄13 24⋄15 26]
m←2 3⍴⍳6 ⋄ (+/÷≢)⍤1m                         ⍝ 1 4

Forms that read worse

Each pair gives the same result.

Reads worse Reads better
{(+/⍵)÷≢⍵} +/÷≢
0.5×⊢+2÷⊢ ⊽⁻¹+÷
{⍵÷+/⍵} ⊢÷+/
{⍵×10}@[1 3] (10×)@[1 3]
{+/⍵}⍠0 +/₀
{⍵,1} ,↢1
×⍨x x²
≥⍣3⊢5 ≥⍣3⁝5
+/⍠i⊢m +/⍠i⁝m

A dfn still reads better where its body needs local names or guards, or where a tacit form would be longer or clearly awkward. Style covers grouping and spacing.
