    . — Determinant / Inner product

    Dyadic operator. Hanzi: 内 (內)

    X f.g Y pairs items with g, then reduces with f. It contracts the last axis of X with the first of Y.

    1 2 3+.×4 5 6      ⍝ 32
[1 0◇0 1]+.×2 2ρι4ₓ ⍝ 2 2ρι4

    Singleton contraction axes extend. Empty contractions use the reduction identity. g⊗ is outer product.

    Float and complex +.× read a large operand that is a transpose, or windows of an array, without copying it. A transpose goes to the matrix product where it lies. Windows are read a block of rows at a time, as Windows describes.

    Where g is a dfn, X f.g Y calls it once on all the pairings together, in a batched call. The result is the same as from one call for each pairing.

    Rank and Axis can give f.g cells of rank 1. Where g is pervasive, g pairs the cells’ items in one call and f folds along them. X+.×¹Y gives the dot product of each pair of rows at the cost of +/X×Y. Axis selects an axis that •index names wherever each argument holds it. The other named axes pair by name. xu.[b k]+.×⍠k⁝wu.[k o] is then the matrix product in index notation. A large one runs as one product.

    X←[1 2 3◇4 5 6] ◇ X+.×¹X             ⍝ 14 77
xu←[2 3]ρι6ₓ ◇ wu←[3 4]ρι12ₓ ◇ [b o k]←•index 2 4 3 ◇ ∧/,(xu.[b k]+.×⍠k⁝wu.[k o])=xu+.×wu   ⍝ $t

    f.g Y, with no left argument, expands the matrix Y by minors along its first column, as J’s u .v does. -.× gives the determinant, and +.× the permanent.

    -.×[1 2◇3 4]ₓ      ⍝ ¯2ₓ
+.×[1 2◇3 4]ₓ      ⍝ 10ₓ
⌈.⌊[1 5◇4 2]       ⍝ 4

    The expansion is C f.g D, where C is the matrix’s first column and D holds the expansions of its minors. Each minor leaves out the first column and one row. A minor that the expansion meets more than once is expanded only once. f and g run once for each distinct minor. A matrix with no columns gives g/ of no items, g‘s identity. Every expansion ends there. g therefore needs an identity, as in J. A vector counts as one column. A number counts as a 1-by-1 matrix. Over every way to pick one item from each row and column, ⌈.⌊ gives the largest of the picks’ smallest items. The expansion takes time that doubles with each row. It accepts at most 64 rows.

    After an array, .name reads a keyed array value: T.name is "name"⊃T. It also works as an assignment target. Plain assignment creates missing records along the path.

    T←["n":1 "addr":["city":"LA"]]
T.addr.city        ⍝ "LA"
T.n+←1 ◇ T.n       ⍝ 2

    After an array, . followed by brackets indexes it. Each bracket item is a position, an array of positions, : for one whole input axis, ∞ for all remaining axes here, or ¯∞ for a new length-one axis. The new axis consumes no input axis and has no name or keys. The positions on one axis form one item, as in m.[[2 0]]. Arrays of positions pair with each other by broadcasting, as arithmetic pairs its arguments, and as NumPy’s advanced indexing pairs index arrays. Named arrays follow arithmetic’s rule for names: arrays with the same name pair, and arrays with different names build a grid. A named array whose name no other array has selects along the axis with its name. Arrays that share a name take the free axes in order, as Index describes. A position outside its axis or not a whole number reads the array’s fill, as Indexing describes. The result holds one item for each pair, on axes that take the place of the first array. Each : keeps its axis in place, a single position removes its axis, and axes after the last item stay whole. With at most one array, x.[I] reads what [I]⌷x reads. An array whose items are coordinates, as ⍸ gives them, covers the axes its coordinates span and pairs with the other arrays as one array, as Index describes. Index gives the cross product of several arrays, and a path through nested items is Pick. Dot indexing binds as tightly as .name and needs no parentheses inside a larger expression. Paths chain from left to right. The array is evaluated before the index. Indexing shows everyday uses.

    m←3 4ρι12ₓ
m.[1 2]            ⍝ 6ₓ
m.[[2 0]]          ⍝ [8 9 10 11◇0 1 2 3]ₓ
m.[ι2ₓ ¯1]          ⍝ [3 7]ₓ
m.[[0 2] [1 3]]    ⍝ [1 11]ₓ
m.[: [0 2]]        ⍝ [0 2◇4 6◇8 10]ₓ
i←ι3ₓ ◇ (3 3ρι9ₓ).[i i]  ⍝ [0 4 8]ₓ
[r c]←•index 3 4 ◇ m≡m.[r c]   ⍝ $t
m.[[1 $n]]         ⍝ [4 5 6 7◇0 0 0 0]ₓ
m.[⍸m>9]           ⍝ [10 11]ₓ
9,m.[0 1],3        ⍝ 9 1 3

    After a function, .name and .[I] read from the function’s result, as they read from an array: f.name is {(α f ω).name}, and f.[I] is {(α f ω).[I]}, with one argument or two. A path goes on from there, as in ⊢.x.[0]. A name that holds a function, or a path to one, gives an inner product instead, as in +.ops.mul. Under writes back through the read.

    .[I] by itself is a selector function, equivalent to ⊢.[I]. Ordinary operators can modify it: .[I]¨ selects from each item.

    v←10 20 30 40
.[[1 3]] v              ⍝ 20 40
1 2 3 4 +.[[0 2]] v     ⍝ 11 33
10× @.[[1 3]]v          ⍝ 10 200 30 400
T←["a":1 "x":[10 20 30]]
⊢.a¨[T T]               ⍝ 1 1
⊢.x.[[2 0]] T           ⍝ 30 10

    T.<operators>[I] applies those operators to the selector before calling it on T. The first bracket at the current level starts the selectors. Parentheses and dfns keep their contents together. Put a bracket-valued operator operand in parentheses, as in T.⍤([0 1])[I]. Numeric superscripts specify Rank in this position.

    T←[[10 20] [30 40]]
.[1]¨T                    ⍝ 20 40
T.¨[1]                    ⍝ 20 40
m←2 3ρι6ₓ
m.¹[1]                    ⍝ [1 4]ₓ
m.¹ ²[1]                  ⍝ [3 4 5]ₓ
m.⍤(1 2)[1]               ⍝ [3 4 5]ₓ

    A named or parenthesized operator head needs a space after the dot: T. op[I] or T. (op)[I]. Tight T.op[I] keeps its field meaning, and tight T.(expr)[I] keeps its scope meaning. A spaced function head retains its ordinary inner-product meaning.

    T←[[10 20] [30 40]]
op←¨
T. op[1]                  ⍝ 20 40
T. (op)[1]                ⍝ 20 40

    The data subject evaluates first. The selectors evaluate once when the selector function is built, outside its operators. With a function subject, the operators select from that function’s result: f.¨[I] is (.[I]¨)∘f. The function runs once on the original arguments. The function subject binds as tightly as in f.[I]. These selectors also work through Under and as selective assignment targets.

    After a keyed vector T, T.(expr) evaluates expr with each key of T as a name for its item. After a matrix, or any array with keys on its first axis, each key names its major cell. Names that aren’t keys resolve in the current scope, and a key hides a name it shares. A key that holds a function binds as a function. The scope’s own result binds where a subject would, whatever it holds, as a call’s result does: T.f 3 calls the function that field f holds, where T.(f) 3 is a strand of that function and 3. Assignments inside the parentheses stay there, and leave T and the current scope unchanged. Execute does the same for code in text: T⍎"price×qty" is T.(price×qty). A scope doesn’t map over an array of records. Write {ω.(expr)}¨recs for that.

    T←["price":2 3;"qty":10 20]
T.(price×qty)              ⍝ 20 60
rate←0.5 ◇ T.(price×rate)  ⍝ 1 1.5
r←T.(price←0) ◇ T.price    ⍝ 2 3
M←"price" "qty":[2 3◇10 20]
M.(price×qty)              ⍝ 20 60
T←["f":-] ◇ (T.f 3) (T.(f) 3)   ⍝ [¯3 [- 3]]

    A dot followed by a digit is a decimal point: .2 is a number. To index position 2, write v.[2] or the subscript v₂. Assignment through a dot path writes the items it reads, and adds missing keys.

    v←10 20 30
v.[[2 0]]←7 8 ◇ v  ⍝ 8 20 7

    Fast combinations

    BPL computes each of these combinations by an algorithm of its own, in any spelling, instead of the expansion.

    Combination Applies to Benefit
    -.×Y a square matrix of numbers takes one LU factorization for floats and complex numbers, or row reduction for exact numbers, which keeps the result exact
    +.×Y a square matrix of numbers with fewer than 64 rows takes rustymath’s permanent, without the expansion’s arrays of minors 
    m←[2 1 0◇1 3 1◇0 1 4]
(•explain "-.× m").fast.rule         ⍝ ["determinant"]
(•explain "+.× m").fast.rule         ⍝ ["permanent"]
